\begin{abstract}
Voice advisory systems for smallholder farmers usually transcribe the spoken query with automatic speech recognition (ASR) and then run retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) on the transcript. Pest, disease, and pesticide names are the words ASR gets wrong most often, and dialectal speech makes this worse, so a single misheard term can lead to wrong advice. We propose a pipeline that retrieves from speech embeddings instead of transcripts, maps dialect terms to scientific entities, escalates low-confidence queries to a human expert, and runs offline on a consumer laptop. We also give an evaluation protocol for it. No speech corpus exists for our target dialect, so we test a working prototype on demo-scale data with synthetic speech. On correct query text, dialect mapping raises dialectal recall@1 from 0.000 to 0.615. A speech-native adapter trained with dialect-normalized targets reaches 0.127 recall@1, up from chance (0.020), and beats the ASR cascade on dialectal queries (0.141 vs.\ 0.038), but it trails the cascade overall (0.127 vs.\ 0.400). The confidence gate answers 34\% of queries at 0.941 accuracy. The full pipeline needs 1.54~GB of GPU memory, and INT8/INT4 quantization made generation slower on our GPU, not faster.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Extension services bring farming knowledge to smallholders, and in regions with low literacy and patchy connectivity there are too few extension agents to go around. Voice chatbots built on RAG help close this gap. Farmer.Chat \cite{farmerchat2024} and KrishokBondhu \cite{ameen2026krishokbondhu} answer spoken questions in local languages, and SukhaRakshak AI \cite{iwmi2025sukharakshak} covers more than twenty Indian languages. All three transcribe the query first and retrieve on the text.

That first step is where things break. On Indian-language agricultural speech, ASR errors fall disproportionately on domain terms \cite{benchmarkingasr2026}, and dialect and code-mixing push error rates higher still \cite{dialectmatters2026}. If the pest name is misheard, retrieval fetches the wrong passage and the farmer gets the wrong advice.

Speech-native retrieval skips the transcript. SpeechRAG \cite{min2025speechrag} trains a speech encoder to land in the embedding space of a frozen text retriever, and Google's Speech-to-Retrieval (S2R) \cite{google2025s2r} does something similar in production. Neither was tested on dialects or on rare domain entities. Neither maps a farmer's local word for a pest to its standard name either, and agricultural knowledge graphs \cite{wu2026cropgraphrag,kgeditorial2023} only cover standard written terms. Two more gaps matter in the field. Current advisory systems answer every query, even when a wrong answer could cost a season's crop; abstention has been argued for in clinical and financial RAG \cite{dependablerag2026,abstinence2025}, though simple confidence signals can fail in both directions \cite{intrygue2026}. And most RAG work assumes a cloud connection \cite{compactllm2026} that many villages do not have. We found no system that handles all four problems together. This paper asks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} Does speech-native retrieval keep its advantage over ASR-cascaded retrieval under dialect variation and rare domain vocabulary?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} Does a dialect-to-scientific-entity mapping layer improve retrieval for regional or colloquial phrasing?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} Can the pipeline run offline on consumer hardware at acceptable latency and accuracy?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} Does hybrid (lexical + dense) retrieval with dialect mapping beat either method alone on rare domain entities?
\end{itemize}

\noindent Our contributions are four. First, we identify dialect variation combined with rare domain vocabulary as an open gap for speech-native retrieval \cite{min2025speechrag,google2025s2r}. Second, we propose a dialect-to-entity mapping layer built from cheap public resources instead of new dialect recordings. Third, we add a confidence gate that hands uncertain queries to a human expert. Fourth, we scope the whole design to a GPU-limited consumer machine, with cloud GPUs used once, for adapter training. We also build and measure a prototype of the pipeline. Section~\ref{sec:related} covers related work, Section~\ref{sec:arch} the architecture, Section~\ref{sec:setup} the evaluation protocol and prototype, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Section~\ref{sec:concl} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Voice and RAG Advisory for Agriculture}
Farmer.Chat \cite{farmerchat2024} runs in four countries with multilingual, multimodal input. KrishokBondhu \cite{ameen2026krishokbondhu} is the system closest to ours: Bengali farmers ask questions over an ordinary phone call and hear the answer spoken back, and it beat the KisanQRS benchmark \cite{rehman2023kisanqrs} by 44.7\% on a composite quality score. SukhaRakshak AI \cite{iwmi2025sukharakshak} and AgroLLM \cite{agrollm2025} follow the same pattern, and a multi-LLM framework \cite{sawant2026farmeraiframework} benchmarks it. A.A.H.A.R. \cite{dubey2025aahar} pairs an offline quantized LLM with ASR tuned to farm vocabulary and accents. Krishi Sathi \cite{vijayvargia2025krishisathi} reports 97.53\% accuracy for bilingual intent-aware QA. Both still transcribe first and adapt only the ASR model to dialect. A Bengali case study \cite{hossain2026bengalirag} bridges colloquial and scientific terms by keyword injection, and its authors list ASR integration and dialect-aware normalization of spoken queries as future work. None of this work tries to fix ASR-driven retrieval failure by changing how retrieval works.

\subsection{ASR Errors and Speech-Native Retrieval}
A benchmark of Indian-language agricultural ASR \cite{benchmarkingasr2026} found that plain word error rate under-penalizes mistakes on crop, chemical, and pest names. A study of cross-lingual transfer \cite{dialectmatters2026} measured how code-mixing hurts transcription for dialects with no dedicated data. Some varieties do have corpora, for example 250 hours of Chhattisgarhi speech \cite{singh2023respin}, but coverage is thin. Speech-native retrieval builds on self-supervised encoders such as wav2vec~2.0 \cite{baevski2020wav2vec} and HuBERT \cite{hsu2021hubert}, on textless spoken language modeling \cite{spidr2025}, and on multilingual models like SeamlessM4T \cite{barrault2023seamlessm4t}. SpeechRAG \cite{min2025speechrag} and S2R \cite{google2025s2r} were evaluated on general queries. Neither targets agriculture or dialect.

\subsection{Entity Mapping, Confidence, and Offline Use}
Dense retrieval does better on paraphrases, and lexical retrieval does better on exact rare entities \cite{sultania2024hybridsearch}, which argues for fusing the two. Mapping colloquial descriptions to formal entities works in text, through soft-prompt tuning over agricultural knowledge graphs \cite{liu2025colloquialpest,wu2026cropgraphrag,yao2024agcner,kgeditorial2023} or through keyword injection \cite{hossain2026bengalirag}, but nobody has done it on speech. For confidence, Dependable RAG \cite{dependablerag2026} and activation-based abstention \cite{abstinence2025} make the case in high-stakes domains, while INTRYGUE \cite{intrygue2026} shows entropy signals flagging correct answers as uncertain, so any gate has to be validated. On devices, 4-bit Llama-3~8B runs at 2--3 tokens per second on 6~GB-RAM phones \cite{compactllm2026}. A.A.H.A.R. and the Bengali study \cite{dubey2025aahar,hossain2026bengalirag} already run text RAG fully on local hardware, and a plant-disease detector in rural Tigray \cite{tigray2025plantdisease} does the same for images. We did not find a system that combines all four parts, and the closest text system \cite{hossain2026bengalirag} names that combination as its own next step.

\subsection{Speech-Native Retrieval}\label{sec:speech}
As in SpeechRAG \cite{min2025speechrag} and S2R \cite{google2025s2r}, a small adapter projects the output of a pretrained speech encoder into the embedding space of a frozen text retriever, which extends dense passage retrieval \cite{karpukhin2020dpr} to spoken queries. The encoder can be wav2vec~2.0 \cite{baevski2020wav2vec}, HuBERT \cite{hsu2021hubert}, or SeamlessM4T \cite{barrault2023seamlessm4t} when the dialect has no pretraining data of its own. Training the adapter is the only step allowed to use a cloud GPU. Inference is local. Unlike earlier work, we train and test with rare entities and dialectal or code-mixed phrasing in mind, because each one hurts ASR on its own \cite{benchmarkingasr2026,dialectmatters2026} and we expect them to compound.

\subsection{Dialect-to-Entity Mapping}\label{sec:dialect}
The mapping layer is a lexicon from dialect terms to scientific entities. We bootstrap it from public resources such as agricultural knowledge graphs \cite{wu2026cropgraphrag}, named-entity datasets \cite{yao2024agcner}, and ontologies \cite{kgeditorial2023}. No new dialect speech is collected. The idea follows \cite{liu2025colloquialpest,hossain2026bengalirag}, which work on written text. Our preferred path applies the mapping in embedding space, so the speech representation of a dialect term lands near its scientific entity. A text-normalization path after ASR is kept only as a fallback, since it brings the transcript back.

\subsection{Hybrid Retrieval}\label{sec:hybrid}
Dense retrieval handles paraphrase well. Lexical retrieval wins on exact, rare entity names \cite{sultania2024hybridsearch}, and those are the pesticide and disease names where mistakes are dangerous. So we fuse both. A pure ``embeddings beat keywords'' design would risk losing exactly that category.

\subsection{Confidence-Gated Escalation}\label{sec:gate}
A wrong recommendation can ruin a crop, so a gate sits between retrieval and generation, as argued for other high-stakes RAG systems \cite{abstinence2025}. Following the taxonomy of Dependable RAG \cite{dependablerag2026}, it combines two kinds of signal. A closed-book signal reflects how sure the system is before it looks at the answer, and an open-book signal checks whether the answer agrees with the retrieved context. Single signals can misfire \cite{intrygue2026}, so we validate the gate on labeled data before trusting it (\S\ref{sec:protocol}). Queries below threshold go to a human expert, much like the call-center fallback in KrishokBondhu \cite{ameen2026krishokbondhu}.

\subsection{Offline Deployment}\label{sec:offline}
After adapter training, no stage needs a network connection. This matches offline text RAG for agriculture \cite{dubey2025aahar,hossain2026bengalirag} and an offline vision system \cite{tigray2025plantdisease}, within the limits reported for quantized on-device LLMs \cite{compactllm2026}. Whether those limits still hold once a speech encoder, adapter, mapping layer, and gate sit in front of generation is the question behind RQ3.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}
The full protocol has not yet been run on the target dialect. It needs a query set split two ways, by phrasing (standard or dialectal) and by entity (common or rare), informed by studies of real farmer helpline queries \cite{godara2022querycount,godara2020sequentialpattern}. Where a dialect corpus exists, such as RESPIN Chhattisgarhi \cite{singh2023respin}, we use it. Otherwise we collect a small test set for the dialectal and rare strata, and no large training corpus. We compare four retrieval conditions: the usual ASR cascade, where Whisper \cite{radford2023whisper} transcribes and text retrieval follows, BM25, dense retrieval \cite{karpukhin2020dpr}, and hybrid retrieval, which we run with and without dialect mapping. We report recall@$k$ and MRR per stratum, not as one average, for the same reason the agriculture-weighted WER of \cite{benchmarkingasr2026} exists: averages hide the failures that matter. The gate is scored against correctness labels on a held-out set before it counts toward any end-to-end result \cite{dependablerag2026,intrygue2026}. All components are timed on one stated hardware setup.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\textit{RQ1:} speech-native retrieval matches or beats the ASR cascade overall, and the gap grows on dialectal and rare strata, where ASR errors pile up \cite{benchmarkingasr2026,dialectmatters2026}. A gap that shrinks there would mean the adapter does not generalize to dialect. \textit{RQ2:} mapping helps dialectal queries and leaves standard ones about the same. An equal gain on both would point to a general effect. \textit{RQ3:} the pipeline meets a fixed latency budget on the stated hardware without losing accuracy. Missing the budget, or a large loss under quantization, would answer RQ3 in the negative. \textit{RQ4:} hybrid retrieval with mapping beats BM25 alone and dense alone on rare entities \cite{sultania2024hybridsearch}. If either one matches it, fusion is not worth the complexity.

\subsection{Prototype and Data}
We built a prototype of Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture} and ran it end to end. Its data is small and written by hand. There is a 42-passage knowledge base on common and rare pests, diseases, and pesticides, a 29-entry dialect lexicon, and 50 test queries. The queries follow the 2$\times$2 design above, crossing standard and dialectal phrasing with common and rare entities. There is no recorded speech for the target dialect, so all audio is synthesized with \texttt{espeak-ng}. These results test the mechanism. They do not answer RQ1--RQ4 for real dialect speech.

The retriever fuses BM25 and dense scores with Reciprocal Rank Fusion ($k{=}60$). The speech-native adapter sits on frozen wav2vec2 hidden states \cite{baevski2020wav2vec} and is trained to reproduce the text retriever's sentence embeddings \cite{min2025speechrag,google2025s2r}. The ASR baseline uses \texttt{faster-whisper} ``tiny'' \cite{radford2023whisper}. The gate follows \cite{dependablerag2026}, and Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct in fp16 generates the answers. Everything ran on one NVIDIA RTX~3050 Laptop GPU with 6~GB of VRAM, and the code passes 24 unit and smoke tests.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\textit{Retrieval.} Dense retrieval uses all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384 dimensions). For each query we take the top 20 candidates from BM25 and from dense search and fuse them with Reciprocal Rank Fusion:
\begin{equation}
s(d) = \sum_{r \in \{\mathrm{bm25},\,\mathrm{dense}\}} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)}, \qquad k = 60.
\end{equation}
\textit{Dialect mapping.} The mapper scans the query for lexicon terms, longest first, and appends the scientific entity names of every match to the query text. So a query that says ``gulabi sundi'' also carries ``pink bollworm''.

\textit{Speech adapter.} We mean-pool the 768-dimensional last hidden states of wav2vec2-base-960h over time. A two-layer MLP (512 hidden units, GELU, LayerNorm) maps the result to a 384-dimensional unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ that lives in the same space as the text embeddings $\mathbf{t}$. Training minimizes in-batch InfoNCE plus a cosine term,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \log \frac{e^{\mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{t}_i/\tau}}{\sum_{j=1}^{B} e^{\mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{t}_j/\tau}} + \frac{1}{B}\sum_{i=1}^{B} \left(1 - \mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{t}_i\right),
\end{equation}
with $\tau = 0.07$. The cosine term matters because several voice variants of one sentence can share a batch, and InfoNCE alone would push them apart. We train for 150 epochs on 843 clips with AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay 0.05, batch size 64).

\textit{Confidence gate.} Let $m$ be the gap between the top-1 and top-2 fused scores and $g$ the share of the answer's content words that also occur in the retrieved passage. The gate computes
\begin{equation}
c = 0.5\,\frac{m}{m + 0.01} + 0.5\,g
\end{equation}
and escalates when $c$ falls below a threshold $\theta$. We pick $\theta$ from a sweep as the most accurate setting that still answers at least five queries.

\section{Results and Discussion}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Retrieval and Dialect Mapping}
Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} gives recall@1 for each condition, and Fig.~\ref{fig:retrieval-chart} plots the standard/dialectal split for the text-input runs. Dialect mapping made the biggest difference we measured. Without it, every text-input condition scores at or near zero on dialectal queries. With it, dialectal recall@1 jumps to 0.615. That is the largest effect in the study, and it supports RQ2 at demo scale. The ASR cascade falls to 0.038 on dialectal queries, and it even trails text-only hybrid retrieval on standard phrasing, in line with \cite{benchmarkingasr2026,dialectmatters2026}. The rare-entity stratum gives a more careful answer to RQ4. Hybrid retrieval with mapping reaches 0.80 recall@1 on rare entities, against 0.52 for BM25 and 0.40 for dense retrieval. Without mapping, though, the hybrid scores 0.48, slightly below BM25. On this small knowledge base the gain comes from mapping, and fusion on its own adds little.

\subsection{Speech-Native Adapter}
Our first adapter was trained on 108 short English templates aligned to passage embeddings. It scored 61.1\% on its own validation templates and 0.020 recall@1 on the test queries, which is chance. It had memorized phrasing. We retrained it on knowledge-base sentences, entity-alias templates, and romanized Hindi templates built from lexicon terms, each synthesized with three voice and speed settings. The training target is the text embedding of the clip's own sentence \cite{min2025speechrag}. For dialectal clips we use the embedding of the lexicon-normalized sentence instead, which puts the mapping of \S\ref{sec:dialect} into embedding space with no transcript at test time. Held-out recall@1 rose to 0.127 (three-seed mean). With raw dialectal targets, dialectal recall@1 drops from 0.141 to 0.038, and normalization won on every seed, though raw targets did better on standard phrasing (0.194 vs.\ 0.111). So on dialectal queries the adapter beats the cascade, as RQ1 predicts. Overall it is still far behind (0.127 vs.\ 0.400). With 26 dialectal queries and synthetic audio we read this as a hint, not evidence for RQ1.

\subsection{Confidence Gate}
We first validated the gate on 50 examples with templated answers. Of these, 78\% were correct, so always answering gives 0.780 accuracy. At its best threshold it answered 32\% of queries at 0.875 accuracy and escalated 81.8\% of the wrong ones. The open-book signal looked useless, though. It stayed between 0.868 and 0.937 whether the answer was right or not. The cause was the templates, which quote the retrieved passage word for word and so always look grounded. We relabeled the same 50 queries with answers generated by the LLM (Table~\ref{tab:gate}). Grounding overlap then spanned 0.000 to 1.000, and the gate answered 34\% of queries at 0.941 accuracy, $+0.161$ over always answering, while catching 90.9\% of wrong answers. The weak signal came from how we built the first test set, not from the signal.

\subsection{Latency and Quantization}
Table~\ref{tab:latency} lists per-stage latency. Generation dominates. Dialect mapping takes 0.1~ms, hybrid retrieval 3.1~ms, and gate scoring under 0.1~ms, against about 1.15~s for an LLM answer of at most 120 tokens. The speech-native path is also cheaper. Encoding plus retrieval took 40.5~ms, while Whisper needed 180.5~ms just to transcribe. With every model loaded, peak GPU memory was 1.54~GB, far below the 6~GB budget. Quantizing the LLM cut its memory from 0.95~GB in fp16 to 0.47~GB in INT4, as expected. It also made generation slower, as Fig.~\ref{fig:quant-chart} shows. We did not expect that. Over three runs, INT8 took about 5.3 times as long as fp16 (6.0--6.1~s against 1.15~s), and INT4 was 34--36\% slower. This runs against the speedups \cite{compactllm2026} reports on phones. Our guess is that the outlier-decomposition matrix multiply in \texttt{bitsandbytes} costs more than it saves at this small batch size and short output length. For RQ3, then, the offline result holds for fp16 on this GPU.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
Table~\ref{tab:rq} sums up where each research question stands. The prototype shows the architecture can be built and that its parts work together. On demo data the results lean the way we hypothesized for dialect mapping, which is RQ2, and for offline latency, which is RQ3. It also exposed a problem we could not have found on paper. The speech-native adapter generalizes poorly, and retraining with embedding-space mapping narrowed that gap without closing it. One worry turned out to be an artifact, since the gate's open-book signal only looked weak because of how the first labels were made. The limits are plain. The knowledge base has only 42 passages. All of the speech is synthetic. We used a small LLM and a single GPU. None of the results show what happens on real dialect speech, so RQ1--RQ4 remain open for the target dialect.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:concl}
No agricultural advisory system we know of combines speech-native retrieval, dialect-to-entity mapping, confidence-gated escalation, and offline operation, although each piece exists on its own \cite{min2025speechrag,google2025s2r,liu2025colloquialpest,hossain2026bengalirag,abstinence2025,dubey2025aahar}. We put the four into one pipeline, wrote down how to test it and what result would count against each hypothesis, and measured a working prototype. Dialect mapping helped the most. The confidence gate held up once it was validated on realistic answers. The speech-native adapter remains the weak point, at 0.127 recall@1. We report the negative results next to the positive ones so the protocol keeps us honest. The next step is the protocol itself, on real data. That means a dialect-stratified test set for the target dialect and an adapter and mapping layer retrained on recorded speech. We also want to see whether a learned alignment can replace the lexicon in embedding space.
